There is a Menorca that only appears when the sky closes in. The coves lose their argument, the terraces are packed away and, quite suddenly, the island shows its other half: the one of cloisters, maturing rooms, copper stills and dining rooms where nobody checks the time. It isn't a lesser version; it's the same island with the lights dimmed and the volume turned down further still.

This guide is for that day. No tricks: every plan here is **genuinely under cover** — we've checked, entry by entry — and whatever happens in the open air is stated plainly at the end, so you don't spend a wet morning somewhere that needs a horizon. Because the [Menorca of all 365 days](/en/article/when-to-visit-menorca/) isn't a slogan: it's a way of being on the island even when the Mediterranean decides to rain.

## Key facts

- **Rain doesn't close the island:** museums, covered markets, tastings and spas run all year round.
- **Booking:** the cheese farms, the winery and the spa need **reserving ahead**; the distillery and the markets don't.
- **Where the rainy-day plans cluster:** **Maó** (museum, distillery, markets) and **Ciutadella** (cathedral, museums, arcades, spa).
- **Honesty first:** Talayotic Menorca, Líthica and the Camí de Cavalls are **open-air**; save them for when it clears.
- **Opening times:** almost everything is seasonal; **check current hours** on each place page before heading out.

## 1. A spa morning under the vault of a grotto

If one plan can make peace with a grey sky, it's this. The [spa at the Faustino Gran](/en/place/spa-faustino-gran-en/), inside a 16th-century palace in Ciutadella's old quarter, keeps its hammam and covered pool beneath the vault of an ancient grotto: stone, water and silence, with a sauna, steam bath and treatment cabins. In there, the rain stops being a problem and becomes the soundtrack. It requires **booking ahead**, so call as soon as you see the forecast.

## 2. The Museu de Menorca: the whole island under one roof

A rainy day is the best excuse to do what nobody does in August: understand the island before travelling it. The [Museu de Menorca](/en/place/museu-de-menorca-en/), in Maó's former convent of Sant Francesc (18th century, with a lovely Baroque cloister), holds the island's largest archaeological collection — more than 36,000 pieces — and traces the full thread, from the Talayotic culture to the British legacy. Ninety minutes well spent, which turn your future visit to [Talayotic Menorca](/en/article/talayotic-menorca/) into something else: recognising, not just looking.

## 3. Ciutadella under cover: arcades, the Cathedral and Can Saura

[Ciutadella](/en/article/ciutadella-city-of-palaces/) is the Menorcan city that best absorbs a grey day, because much of its charm happens under a roof or under an arch. The whitewashed arcades of **Ses Voltes** let you walk the centre with the umbrella barely open; the [Cathedral](/en/place/catedral-de-santa-maria-ciutadella-en/), Gothic and hushed, is visited on a single ticket that includes its museum and the Convent de Sant Agustí; and the [Museu de Ciutadella, in the Can Saura palace](/en/place/museu-de-ciutadella-can-saura-en/), lays out the city's archaeology across fifteen rooms. Finish at [the market](/en/place/mercat-municipal-de-ciutadella-en/) on Plaça de la Llibertat, with its central fish hall under 19th-century ironwork and tapas counters through the morning. Hours are seasonal everywhere: confirm before you go.

## 4. The Gin Xoriguer distillery, on Maó's harbour

Few doorways on the island smell better on a rainy day. At the [Gin Xoriguer distillery](/en/place/destileria-gin-xoriguer-en/), beside the port of Maó, [Menorca's gin](/en/article/menorca-gin/) is still made in copper stills with more than two centuries of history, from wine alcohol and juniper berries. The visit ends where it should: at the tasting, with the gins, the artisan liqueurs and the *pomada* — gin with cloudy lemonade — that explains the island's festes. No booking needed, and it sits a short walk from the markets: the natural first piece of a Maó morning under cover.

## 5. Inside a cheese farm: Subaida or Sant Patrici

[Mahón-Menorca DOP cheese](/en/article/mahon-menorca-cheese/) only fully makes sense the day you see the wheels resting on their shelves. At the [Subaida cheese farm](/en/place/queseria-subaida-en/), a 350-hectare estate producing around 80 wheels of artisan cheese a day, the visit includes a making demonstration, the maturing room and a tasting of three varieties with sobrasada, almond sweets and wine. At [Hort de Sant Patrici](/en/place/hort-de-sant-patrici-en/), in Ferreries, add a cheese museum — open since 1999 in a former farm building — a winery of its own and one of the best-stocked gourmet shops on the island. Both require **booking**; both are worth the detour.

## 6. Tapas under a cloister: Maó's covered markets

Market life is the definitive rainy-day plan, and [Maó](/en/article/mahon-natural-harbour/) has two adjoining doors. The [Mercat des Claustre del Carme](/en/place/mercat-claustre-carme-en/) occupies an 18th-century Carmelite cloister in the heart of town: local produce, home-style cooking and crafts beneath its galleries, Monday to Saturday. And the [Mercat des Peix](/en/place/mercat-des-peix-en/), the old fish market of 1927, is now a food hall with a local soul: fish and shellfish in tapas form, island wines, and the whole city passing along the counter. No booking, no hurry, no getting wet.

## 7. The island in a glass: the Binifadet winery

A wet afternoon is a good moment to take [Menorca's wine](/en/article/menorca-wine/) seriously. [Bodegas Binifadet](/en/place/bodegas-binifadet-en/), in Sant Lluís, is the reference house: the island's oldest and largest producer — around 100,000 bottles a year, and the only one making sparkling wine — under the IGP Vi de la Terra Illa de Menorca. The explanation of the process and the tasting happen indoors; the walk among the vines will, naturally, depend on the sky. Book the visit and, if the plan stretches on, a table at its restaurant too.

## 8. The long lunch

There remains the oldest rainy-day plan in the world: eating slowly. Menorca's [discreet fine dining](/en/article/menorca-fine-dining/) — small dining rooms, fish-market and garden produce, the DOP cheese at every stage of maturing — is built for exactly this: a lunch that competes with no cove, because on that day no cove is competing. Two honest warnings: the best houses have very few tables and **booking is essential**; and in low season many close or cut their days, so call ahead.

## What to save for when it clears

Being useful also means saying what **doesn't** work in the rain. The settlements of [Talayotic Menorca](/en/article/talayotic-menorca/) are open-air sites; [Líthica](/en/article/lithica-quarries-ciutadella/), for all its "stone cathedral" name, is a quarry of open-sky chambers; and a muddy [Camí de Cavalls](/en/article/the-cami-de-cavalls/) is a plan for a different kind of traveller. The [Cova d'en Xoroi](/en/article/cova-den-xoroi-clifftop-cave/) shelters under the rock, but its essence — the terraces and the sunset over the sea — asks for a clean horizon. None of these places disappears: they'll be waiting, improved, the next day.

## Our take

A rainy day in Menorca is not a lost day; it's a different one. Our recipe: a morning of museum or market, a tasting with no clock, a long table — and the smell of wet earth when you step outside. The island you discover this way — the one of cloisters, wineries and maturing rooms — is precisely the one the August traveller never gets to see.
